This site is a week old. Its repository has 29 commits and 27 of them carry a co-author line
for Anthropic's Claude coding agent. I didn't write most of the CSS, name most of the variables,
or type most of the build scripts.

So what did I do?

It's a fair question, and everyone who works with agents will eventually have to answer it. Not to
defend themselves, but because the answer is where the leverage is. If you know which part of the
work is yours, you can get better at it. If you don't, you'll spend your time competing with a tool
at the things it's already better at.

## We've been here before

In 1954, the team building FORTRAN wrote that it "should virtually eliminate coding and
debugging." Their 1957 paper called it an "automatic coding system." It did eliminate something:
writing machine instructions by hand. Programmers didn't go away. They moved up a level and built
things nobody could have built before. Every layer since, from compilers to frameworks to package
managers, has taken a share of the typing and left the job in place.

Fred Brooks drew the line in 1986. Some of software's difficulty is *accidental*: syntax, tooling,
the friction of turning an idea into something a machine accepts. The rest is *essential*: "The
hardest single part of building a software system is deciding precisely what to build." Agents are
extraordinary at the accidental part. They're getting good at a lot of what used to feel essential,
too. The line keeps moving, which is why I don't think the usual question is the right one.

## Don't ask what it can't do

The tempting first move is to list what AI can't do. That's where I started: an agent won't have a great
idea in the middle of the night, and it won't fix a bug unless someone tells it to.

The trouble is that list is shrinking faster and faster. It's not uncommon as of writing this for
agents to run on schedules, watch CI, open their own pull requests, even manage their own
asynchronous teams. My list of things AI can't do quickly became a list of things it probably
will do soon.

A sturdier question is: what only means something when a person does it? J.C.R. Licklider answered
it in 1960, in a paper about how people and computers might work together: "men will set the
goals, formulate the hypotheses, determine the criteria, and perform the evaluations," while
"computing machines will do the routinizable work that must be done to prepare the way for
insights." Sixty-six years later, the routinizable share has grown beyond anything he could have
pictured. His list for the humans still holds, and I'd boil it down to three things: wanting,
judging and answering.

## Wanting

A crane does all of the lifting on a job site, but none of the deciding. It isn't missing strength.
It's missing a reason to lift. That's what the operator brings.

Agents are the same, and that holds even for the ones that run on their own. A scheduled agent is
running because someone wanted it to. Initiative isn't a feature you can bolt on; it comes from
caring how something turns out.

Humans are uniquely gifted at wanting things. Like it or not, it's one of the hard differentiators
between human intelligence and artificial intelligence. Desire is fundamentally emotional, chaotic,
illogical, spontaneous. It justifies itself, and so cannot be wrong or right. An agent that displayed
those properties would be written off as inconsistent, unreliable, unpredictable: no one would use a
crane that decides mid-lift it wants to drop the load somewhere else.

Nothing told me to make this site - I just *wanted* to. It came out of a curiosity to ship a site
as simply as possible; a need for a personal portfolio with a URL; a craving to host and share my
own lab experiments.

No agent wanted any of this at all, and nothing could have been built without my human impetus.
That isn't a capability gap. There simply was no one on the other side to want those things.

## Judging

The best ideas, songs and inventions tend to come out of messy, human processes, and they often
carry the fingerprints of whoever made them. That mess is exactly what we train out of AI.

The fine-tuning that makes models helpful and reliable, reinforcement learning from human
feedback, "significantly reduces output diversity," according to one study of its effects. The same
pull shows up in the people using them. In a 2024 experiment, 300 writers wrote short stories, some
with ideas from a language model and some without. The AI-assisted stories were rated more
creative, especially for the least creative writers. They were also more like one another. Each
writer did better, and the group as a whole got more alike.

That isn't a bug so much as a choice. A coding agent that improvises is a liability, so we build
these systems to be consistent, correct and never weird. It's a little like making them in the
image of who we'd like to be. Which leaves the weird all to us.

Judging is the person deciding whether *correct* is also *good*, and sometimes that the strange
option is the right one. Similar to a desire, a judgement is self-justifying: it may be based on
something measurable, or it may be based on arbitrary taste - neither is necessarily wrong.

## Answering

In 1985, Peter Naur argued that programming is really "an activity by which the programmers form
or achieve a certain kind of insight," a theory of the problem and its solution. The code is only
a trace of that theory. He arrived at an uncomfortable conclusion: "The death of a program happens
when the programmer team possessing its theory is dissolved," and rebuilding the theory "merely from
the documentation, is strictly impossible."

Agents make this sharper. An agent starts each session knowing only what it can read. The theory
has to live somewhere, and the only place it can live in full is the person.

There's a slide, said to come from an IBM training session in 1979, that has been making the rounds
for years:

> A computer can never be held accountable. Therefore a computer must never make a management
> decision.

No one has found the original. IBM's archivists looked and came up empty, and the only known copy
was lost in a flood. People keep passing it along anyway, because it names something everyone who
has worked in software development already knows.

When I merge a pull request, I'm saying I understand the change well enough to answer for it. The
agent can't be on the hook for that. Someone has to be.

## The catch

In 1983, Lisanne Bainbridge published a short paper on industrial control rooms called *Ironies of
Automation*. Its central irony: "the more advanced a control system is, so the more crucial may be
the contribution of the human operator." Automation takes the easy work and "leaves the operator to
do the tasks which the designer cannot think how to automate." Meanwhile the operator gets less
practice, and "skills deteriorate when they are not used."

That's the real risk in the idea that developers become managers of agents. A manager who stops
reading the code loses the theory. Without the theory, they can't judge. Without judgment, they
can't answer for the work.

We're also bad at noticing when it happens. In an early-2025 study by METR, experienced open-source
developers expected AI tools to speed them up by 24%. With the tools, they took 19% *longer*. Even
afterward, they believed the tools had made them 20% faster. METR has since said that newer tools
probably do speed developers up, so the slowdown is dated. The gap between how it felt and what
happened is the important part. You can't tell whether the arrangement is working by how it
feels.

So I'm not sure "manager" is the right word. "Editor" is closer: someone who doesn't type most of
the words but is still inside the text, reading every line with a sense of responsibility and,
to a degree, ownership.

## The shape of the work

Here's how this site is built, as a specimen. None of it is clever. Each piece is there to keep the
wanting, judging and answering with me.

**The directives are written down.** The repo has one file of agent directives, `AGENTS.md`. It
holds principles, working agreements and house style, and every rule I've added is attributed and
dated:

> **No em dashes** in site copy, docs or PR descriptions; use a colon, a comma or a new sentence
> instead (Sean, 2026-10-12).

That's taste, recorded in a form that outlives a session. The agent reads it before it reads
anything else.

**What's next fits in a few lines.** `docs/STATUS.md` opens with "Keep this short: point to the
next slice." The real plans live in a working folder, with one rule for when they go stale: "When
it disagrees with the code, the code wins."

**The theory is written down too, where it can be.** `AGENTS.md` has a section called "Things the
code knows that you don't." It's where lessons go after they cost something. One reads: "both
collisions so far shipped broken layouts into review." Naur is right that documentation can't
replace the theory. It can give the agent enough to work inside a theory I still hold.

**One slice per branch, one pull request per slice.** Small enough that I can read the whole diff
and keep the whole change in my head. The agent opens the pull request without asking.

**I merge.** Merging to `main` deploys the site. It's the one step that changes what the world
sees, and it's the one the agent doesn't take.

**Claims trace to sources.** It's a principle in the file, and it applies to this essay too. Every
quote above is checked against its source, the one whose original is lost says so, and the sources
are listed below.

## Tool or coworker?

There's an easy line to end on: an intelligent tool is still a tool. There's a less comfortable one
right behind it: an intelligent tool may not be a tool at all. If it isn't, then what is it? A
coworker? Something more?

I don't know. What I notice is that nothing above depends on the answer. Crane or colleague,
someone still has to want the building, judge whether it's any good and sign for it when it's done.
For now, that's my seat.
