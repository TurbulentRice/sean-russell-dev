## The problem

Before this work, production failures on bbb.org were found by users and staff, usually hours or
days after they started. Login broke twice: once from a migration, once from a holiday firewall
rule that turned real sign-ins away. Complaints and reviews stopped rendering on business
profiles while the page still returned 200. Filing a complaint went dark over a holiday weekend,
and nothing noticed.

Every server-side monitor stayed green through all of it. CPU, memory, error rate and liveness
measure whether the servers are healthy, not whether a person can finish what they came to do.

The end-to-end test suite didn't help either. It ran only on stage, through bypass cookies and
headers that production rightly refuses, so it could pass while production was broken. One of
those bypasses had hidden a live failure to save reviews for weeks.

## What I built

The initiative belonged to an engineering lead, and I was its primary engineer: the probe
harness, the CI and alerting layer, and an internal releases app (Fig. 01). An SRE teammate
built five of the form-submission journeys, the alert diagnostics and the scheduler.


The design rests on a few choices:

- **One journey library, two targets.** Each journey is written once and runs against stage after
  every release and against production on a schedule. A guardrail check fails any change that
  lets the two drift, or that adds a bypass or request mocking, so the stage suite can never again
  pass by skipping the parts that break.
- **Probes that behave like strangers.** They run from outside the network, through the real CDN
  and firewall, and are exempted only from the few anti-automation checks a probe can't honestly
  pass. One-time codes arrive through a real inbound mailbox. Eleven journeys are built and nine
  run in production, including login, registration, complaints, reviews, and applying for
  accreditation.
- **Probe data never mixes with real data.** Where a table can flag synthetic rows, probe rows are
  written, flagged, and filtered out of everything that aggregates, publishes, reports, exports or
  trains. Where it can't, the endpoint accepts the request and stores nothing. A probe that leaves
  traces in staff tools is a probe nobody will keep running.
- **Alerts people act on.** A failed journey posts to the on-call channel with which journey
  failed, where it stopped, and the likely cause. Known live defects show in red with their
  ticket instead of being silenced.
- **A home for the evidence.** The releases app streams the private Playwright reports and traces,
  and records every release, deployment and smoke result across apps. It sits on a release tool I
  had written earlier, which the team uses to cut, patch and promote releases across its apps.

## The headline find

Users had been reporting what looked like several unrelated bugs, each on only some page loads
and never in a dev environment: a Submit button that did nothing, with no spinner, error or
request; a "Get listed" link that opened a dead page; a complaint form that bounced back to its
start.

The probes kept failing in ways that looked flaky. Their traces didn't: they showed requests to
`localhost` and a development reCAPTCHA key, on production pages.

</Figure>

When the server render took more than 3 seconds, the page went out without its configuration,
and the browser fell back to the development defaults compiled into the build. About 18 of 33
settings were wrong on such a page. That fallback had existed for at least 13 months.

Nobody could reproduce it because it happened per render, so the next load worked. It clustered
with slowness. Every layer stayed silent, including Sentry, which the same wrong settings had
switched off on exactly those renders. And because each form touched different wrong values, it
had been filed as four or more separate bugs.

I closed it in four steps:

1. Made the fallback degrade safely, with relative links and hardened values.
2. Made it visible. The probes record the fallback rate on every run, which put a number on it:
   2.8% of sampled renders. The alert names the defect.
3. Found a way to trigger the fallback on demand in production, without writing any data, which
   turned an intermittent bug into a deterministic one.
4. Fixed the class at its source. The main application now emits the configuration on every render
   path, and the client fails loudly if it's missing instead of falling back.

The structural fix reached production 18 days after the umbrella ticket was filed. Slow renders
still happen at the same rate, but those pages now work.

## When the dashboards are wrong

The probes are one half of seeing production. The other half is the services reporting on
themselves, and for two years I'd been giving the services our team runs the same three things:
Prometheus metrics, a health endpoint, and one shared Sentry setup, including OpenTelemetry
instrumentation of the server-render service.

That paid off on a Sunday in September 2026, when the probes failed 19 runs in a row over four
hours while every dashboard stayed green. The first theory was a readiness problem in the render
service. The render metrics said otherwise:

- The render service stayed fast throughout. Its p95 went from 48 to about 80 ms, against a
  3,000 ms budget.
- Meanwhile the main application's open sockets per pod grew from about 450 to about 16,000.
- A shared header-and-footer service was crash-looping 40 to 60 times an hour.

Together they told the story. Calls to the crashing service were exhausting the main
application's outbound connections, so the render request never got a connection in time and
never appeared in the render service's metrics at all. The same evidence showed that the first
proposed remedy wouldn't have prevented the outage. And it connected back to the headline find: a
starved render is exactly what triggered the configuration fallback.

## Other catches

The probes have produced 19 tickets so far, about 12 of them distinct user-facing defects. Among
them: a race in the accreditation application that told some users there was no matching BBB,
where I fixed the cause that blocked real users before the noisier one behind most of the alerts;
and account pages that returned 404 for one to three minutes on every rolling deploy, caught 25
seconds after a rollout began.

They also audited the monitoring itself, exposing alert emails the chat tool silently dropped, a
concurrency setting that cancelled one journey on every run, and a digest that threw away every
diagnostic.

## What I'd do differently

Measure the business side from the start. We know how often the configuration fallback broke a
page and how much the fix reduced failures, but not how many complaints, reviews and applications
it cost or recovered. That number would have made the case for this work on its own.
