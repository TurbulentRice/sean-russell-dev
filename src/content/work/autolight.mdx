## The problem

A wedding photographer can come home with four thousand frames, and most of the hours after
that go to editing them. Most of that editing is the same handful of habits: this much
exposure, that tone curve, these colors pulled back. A photographer's own Lightroom catalogs
already hold years of those decisions.

AutoLight learns from them. Point it at catalogs you've already edited and it trains a model on
your slider habits. Then hand it a new shoot and it delivers edits back into Lightroom for you
to review. It runs entirely on your machine: no presets, no uploads, and your RAW files never
move.

## Constraints

- **The catalog is sacred.** A Lightroom catalog is an undocumented SQLite database holding
  years of a photographer's work. Damaging one is not a bug; it's the end of trust.
- **Local only.** No cloud training, no account, and it has to work offline.
- **One person.** Every moving part has to earn its maintenance cost.
- **The model sees very little.** It learns from a small preview of each photo, not the RAW, so
  anything a preview can't show, it can't learn.

## Decisions

### Deliver into Lightroom, not beside it

The obvious design renders edited photos in the app and lets you approve them. I didn't build
that. An in-app renderer would never match Lightroom's own, and it would fail quietly: a
photo that looks slightly wrong is worse than one that's plainly missing. Catalog writes, on
the other hand, are testable, deterministic, and fail loudly.

So AutoLight writes its edits into the catalog as Lightroom's own **virtual copies**, grouped
in a dated collection, and you review them in the tool you already know. Lightroom doesn't
document how a virtual copy is stored, so I worked it out by diffing the one real virtual copy
in a test catalog against its master across all 133 tables. A virtual copy turned out to be
rows in about seventeen of them.

Every write goes into a **copy** of the catalog, never the original, and is gated on a SQLite
integrity check plus a row-count reconciliation. A pass that fails its checks is reported as a
failed pass, never as work done. Writing onto the original is possible, but only as an explicit
action that backs the catalog up first. I also considered XMP sidecar files, which are safer to
write, but they need a manual "Read Metadata" step in Lightroom and can't make virtual copies.

### Draw a hard line between the app and the model

The first version was a Python prototype, and the desktop app grew around it. That left two
places that both thought they knew where files lived and what work came next. I drew one rule
and enforced it:

> Python answers "here's the result of the work you asked for." It never answers "what should
> I work on?"

The Python ML core became a stateless worker: absolute paths and parameters in, a stream of
JSONL events out, then exit. Everything about state (what exists, where it lives, which jobs
are running) belongs to the Electron side, behind a typed contract shared by both TypeScript
halves. Retiring the prototype's UI and its duplicated bookkeeping removed about 900 lines.
Jobs persist to SQLite and survive the window closing mid-training.

### Measure before believing

A model that edits photos always *looks* like it's working, because almost any edit looks
plausible on its own. So I built a benchmark that tests the model the way it's really used:
train on some shoots, then edit a shoot it has never seen, and compare against simple
baselines. Every comparison runs against a control, and training is seeded, so two runs on the
same photos produce bit-identical weights. Before that, a difference between two experiments
could be noise.

The benchmark changed the roadmap. Learning a photographer's style *within* one shoot is the
easier part. Carrying it to a shoot the model has never seen is the hard part, and it's where
the work is now.

## What I built

<Figure caption="Three processes on a typed contract, and a one-way path from your catalog to a validated copy." wide scroll>

</Figure>

The app is built around three nouns. A **Style** is a model with an identity: what it learned
from, its versions, and a measured signature of its habits, as in Fig. 01. A **Catalog** is a Lightroom catalog
you've introduced to the app. A **Pass** is one delivery of work: a Style, a catalog, and a
validated result. Drag a catalog onto a Style and choose whether it should learn from it or edit
it.


Lightroom compatibility is tested rather than assumed: every catalog version from Lightroom
Classic 6 to 15 has a real specimen in the test suite, and the reader, the write, and the
"was this photo actually edited?" check are proved against each one. That last check exists
because no catalog field says whether a photo was edited, so AutoLight works it out from the
develop settings themselves and only learns from photos you actually touched.

## Results

The whole loop works end to end: watch a catalog, notice new edits, learn, deliver, review,
correct, learn again. Every step waits for the photographer to say go. Keeping or deleting a
delivered virtual copy feeds back into the next round of learning as confirmation or
correction. AutoLight is in early access; there's a waitlist at
[autolight.ai](https://autolight.ai).

## What I'd do differently

Build the benchmark first. I spent the first year making the loop work end to end, and only
then built the instrument that showed where the model actually stood. It moved the project's
priorities more than any feature did, and it would have moved them sooner.
